I Never Showed It the Task

If you only ever show a robot single skills, can it do a six-step task on its own?

Ayush Deshmukh · Isaac Lab, PPO, SmolVLA

Code Play with it

TL;DR I filmed myself doing single skills on my phone: push, pick up, put down, never two in a row. I turned the clips into a reward, trained an RL teacher with it in simulation and distilled the teacher into a vision-language-action model. Given one sentence with six commands, the model does all six in order in 40 of 100 runs, and four commands in 81 of 100, though no training episode had more than two.

The idea in five steps

  1. Film atomic skillsPush, pick up, put down. One skill per clip.
  2. Infer the rewardThe object's tracked path becomes the reward.
  3. Train an RL teacher16,384 simulated robots learn each skill and the handoff.
  4. Distill into a VLAIt copies the teacher from cameras and the sentence.
  5. Compose new tasksOne sentence, six commands, never demonstrated.

Results

Each task is run on 100 table layouts the model never trained on. A run counts only if the simulator saw every command done, in order. Training episodes had at most two commands, so everything marked NEVER SHOWN is longer than anything the model saw.

40%6 commands (stack, unstack, restack), all in order. Never shown.
81%4 commands (stack, then unstack to the target), all in order. Never shown.

Scoring. Picked up = raised 4 cm. On the target = within 5 cm of its centre. On the other object = within 5 cm and within 3 cm of the right height. Each held for 0.2 s. My first check (1.5 cm, 0.5 s) failed runs where the cube sat on the cylinder a few millimetres off; with it the 6-command score is 12. The teacher is scored the same way.

Why this way?

Why not fine-tune a VLA on the clips?

It doesn't learn to combine skills. Trained directly on single-skill demos, SmolVLA learned some pushing (16 of 100), no picking up, and scored 0 on every combined task.

Why not copy my hand?

A hand and a two-finger gripper grasp differently, and the clips have no gripper commands. What transfers is what happens to the object. RL works out how the gripper makes that happen.

Why not record robot demos of the long tasks?

Every combination would need its own teleoperated demos. I wanted to film each skill once and get the combinations for free.

Why not hand-write a reward per task?

I tried; the policy gamed every one. A reward on the object's path doesn't care how the object is held, and works for any combination.

Why an RL teacher first?

RL on a 450M image model is very slow. A small teacher with exact positions learns every skill in about an hour on 16,384 parallel scenes; the VLA only has to copy it.

Why the whole sentence, not one command at a time?

Feeding commands one by one works and scores higher (90 vs 76 on 4 commands), but then the memory lives in a script. I wanted the model to keep its own place.

1 · Film atomic skills

43 phone clips of me with a real cube and a crisps can: 12 pushes, 10 pick-ups, 21 put-downs. Never two skills in a row, and none of the tested tasks. An ArUco marker on each object and the target gives its position on the table in every frame.

2 · Infer the reward from the clips

The reward only looks at the object. Eight points on it have to follow the skill's template from start to goal, and the robot is paid for progress along it.

  • From the clips: lift height (20 cm), drop height, push direction, how much the object may turn, and the 3 cm tolerance (how much my own clips vary).
  • Set by hand: "finished" = within 2 cm of the goal, slower than 0.1 m/s for 0.3 s; a small step cost, a bonus per command, a small bonus for holding still.
Gripper rewards got gamed. Paying for grasping and carrying, the policy flicked the fingers on the release threshold to collect both terms (0 of 100). Paying for "near the goal", it pushed instead of lifting. Pushing can't put a cube 20 cm in the air, so a path reward can't be gamed that way.
No signal at the start. An object-only reward doesn't change until the gripper touches the object; from the home pose nothing was learned in 504 iterations. The fix is one chain, gripper → object → target, with every episode starting from a random arm pose.
It pushed by lifting. With that chain, the teacher "pushed" by carrying the cube (85 of 100), and its student couldn't tell push from pick up. A small penalty for lifting during a push fixed it (0 of 100).

3 · Train an RL teacher

A Franka Panda in Isaac Lab, PPO on 16,384 parallel scenes, 1000 iterations. It sees the exact object positions and which command it's on. Each episode has one or two commands, enough to learn every skill and the handoff to the next. A 6-step task is five handoffs in a row. Told which command it's on, it does 94 of 100 six-command runs.

A low-pass filter on the teacher's arm. PPO learned bang-bang control that the VLA couldn't imitate (no better than predicting the average action). The arm now executes a running average of the teacher's commands (95% old, 5% new), and the student learns the smooth executed motion. Each step up in smoothing improved the student.

4 · Distill into a VLA

I record the teacher (3000 paired episodes + 1500 pushes, at most two commands each) and train SmolVLA (450M) to copy it from a front camera, a wrist camera and the sentence. No object positions, no command counter.

Every scene is recorded twice. Once saying "the cube" and once "the cylinder". The images are the same, so the model has to read the sentence to pick the object. In an earlier version this took picking the right object from 45 to 80.

The memory problem

A long task needs the skills, the handoffs, and knowing where you are. The image doesn't tell you: in the 6-step task the arm reaches for the cube in step 1 and again in step 3, and the scenes look almost the same. What I tried:

  1. Nothing, just the whole sentence.
    4 commands: 14 of 100, in order once
  2. Show it the past (older frames, past actions).
    no help
  3. An outside pointer, one command at a time.
    4 commands: 90 of 100Works, but the memory is in a script, not the model.
  4. A counter in the input ("k commands done").
    4 commands: 75 · 6 commands: 5The 6th position never came up in training.
  5. "(done)" marks in the sentence itself.
    6 commands: 40 of 100 in orderA language model reads marked words far better than a number.

The model writes the marks itself. Halfway through the 4-step task it reads:

pick up the cube(done), then put the cube on the cylinder(done), then pick up the cube, then put the cube on the target

  • Label: an extra output is 1 for the last half-second before the teacher switched commands.
  • Mark: above 0.85 for five steps → "(done)" after the current command. The first unmarked command is the one to do.
  • Padding: training sentences are padded to 10 commands, some pre-marked, so every position shows up.
  • It uses them: with marks off, 4 commands finish 0 of 50 times. With two marks written in advance, it skips to command three and finishes 42 of 50.

Poking at it

Disturbances it never saw in training (earlier model, 50 layouts each):

  • Knock the finished cube away: it goes back for it 16 of 30 times, reacting to the image rather than its marks.
  • Move the cube while the arm reaches: 12 of 50. Re-targeting a moved object is weak.
  • Take the cube out of the gripper: picked up again 10 of 36 times.

Play with it

The robot runs live on the play page (when my GPU is on). Three games:

  • Sabotage the robot. It gets a 2-, 4- or 6-step task; you get one sabotage per round: knock the object away or write a fake "(done)". You win if the task isn't really done when it stops. To win you have to know how it works: it keeps its place only through its own "(done)" marks and never goes back to a marked step, so the right sabotage at the right moment beats it.
  • Tic-tac-toe. You're O, the robot is X. The game picks its square and moves the green pad there; the robot has to really place a cube on it. A miss loses the turn.
  • Build a sentence. Chain up to ten commands and watch it work through them.

The tic-tac-toe robot is a fine-tune of an earlier student with the same recipe: first on recordings with the target on the nine squares, then on 2,000 more with random flat X and O pieces on the board. With pieces on the board it places its cube 97 of 100 times, against 38 before that second step.

What still goes wrong

The 60 failed six-command runs stop mostly at picking up the cylinder (25), putting the cube on the target (15) and putting the cube on the cylinder (13). 21 of 100 knock something over.

  • 8 commands: 0 of 100 in order. The done signal fires whenever an object is lifted and held still, whatever the command, so the marks run ahead and it stops after four. The teacher does it 100 of 100.
  • The cylinder is the weak skill: pick up 59, to the target 77, onto the cube 81, against 94–97 for the cube. It tips easily, and sentences that start with it mostly fail. Push is 59 too.
  • No undo. A wrong "(done)" is never taken back, so the skipped step is never redone.
  • Put-downs are a little loose. With a 1.5 cm, 0.5 s check the six-command score is 12, not 40.
  • One scene. One cube, one cylinder, one target, two fixed cameras, in simulation. Anything new on the table hurts until it's in the data: flat tic-tac-toe pieces dropped placement to 38 of 100, back to 97 after one fine-tune.

Next

  1. A done judge. A small separate model, image + sentence → "is this command finished?", trained with frames of the other commands as negatives. The action model's flag reads the image, not the text.
  2. RL on the student itself, starting from the distilled model, on the long tasks. The teacher shows the skills are learnable; the student is 3–54 points behind it per task.
  3. Let it undo a mark. A self-check already erases mostly wrong marks (93–100% of them really weren't done); it needs to redo the step too.
  4. More cylinder data, and a teacher that grasps it lower so it doesn't tip.
  5. Varied scenes in the recordings (distractors, textures, light), since one fine-tune on new pieces fixed them.
  6. A real robot. The clips are real; the robot isn't yet.
