No Eyes, No Problem

What Does Touch Contribute to Blind Baoding Ball Manipulation?

[Authors — anonymous during review]

[Affiliations]

Paper arXiv Code Video

TL;DR A PPO policy trained entirely in simulation transfers zero-shot to a physical Shadow Dexterous Hand Lite and performs blind Baoding ball rotation from proprioception and 16 binary tactile signals alone, with no vision, depth, motion capture or object-state estimation. A coupling-aware simulation embodiment, hysteresis-based tactile calibration and two new episodic randomisations, slew-rate limits (SlewDR) and Stuck-AT Taxels (STAT), make the transfer possible. The policy sustains 225 consecutive 180° rotations (112.5 full rotations) at ~0.36 rot/s in a single continuous trial.

Key findings

  • Touch improves exchange completion. With tactile input zeroed at deployment (C3-TacOff), the policy keeps almost the same gait frequency as C3 (~8.1 vs 7.9 cycles), but exchange efficiency drops from η = 0.90 to 0.58. The gait is still executed; many cycles simply fail to produce an exchange. The proprioception-only policy runs one of the fastest gaits yet has the lowest η (0.31), and its joint trajectories are nearly identical with and without balls in the hand. It is replaying a learned cycle rather than adapting to the object.
  • Touch sustains the task. C3 completes ~7× as many rotations as C3-TacOff (34.75 vs 5.0) and stays up ~5× longer (94.8 s vs 19.0 s). Its joint trajectories vary in amplitude from cycle to cycle, a sign of online correction. In one trial the thumb failed to push a ball far enough toward the ring finger; it held the incoming ball while the index and middle fingers repositioned the other, and the gait resumed. In simulation, removing touch raises the drop rate from 2% to 32%.
  • Simulated performance does not predict hardware performance. C1 has the highest simulated speed (0.96 rot/s) but manages only 0.35 full rotations on hardware, with a 70% drop rate. Its large, rapid commands exceed what the tendon-driven hand can actually track.
  • SlewDR makes motion trackable; STAT removes reliance on single taxels. Rate-limiting actions during training (C2) produces smoother commands and brings simulated and hardware speeds closer, but every hardware trial still ends in a drop. When touch is removed from C2 in simulation, its drop rate rises to nearly 60%, showing heavy dependence on continuous tactile input. Adding STAT (C3) raises hardware performance from 0.5 to 34.75 full rotations, and removing touch from C3 raises its simulated drop rate only to 32%. The learned gait becomes the primary driver of motion, with touch providing local correction of the ball configuration.
  • Sparse, approximate touch is enough. On hardware, tactile activation shifts toward the proximal middle- and ring-finger sensors compared with simulation, yet transfer still succeeds. An exact sensor-by-sensor correspondence between simulation and hardware is not required for this task.

Overview

Humans manipulate objects in-hand through touch alone with remarkable ease, but sustained real-world manipulation without vision remains an open challenge for robots. Reinforcement learning can produce sophisticated blind manipulation policies in simulation, yet transferring them to a real dexterous hand is hindered by continuously changing contacts, motion-induced drift in tactile signals, and the gap between simulated and physical dynamics.

We study this problem through Baoding ball rotation on the four-fingered Shadow Dexterous Hand Lite. It is a harder test of in-hand dexterity than single-object reorientation: two free objects circulate simultaneously, interacting with each other and with the fingers, and their phase relationship must be maintained without being observed. The policy receives only proprioception and 16 binary contact signals. Zero-shot transfer is achieved through a sensor-aligned, coupling-aware simulation embodiment, per-channel tactile calibration, and two novel episodic domain randomisations: slew-rate action limits (SlewDR) and Stuck-AT Taxels (STAT).

On the physical hand, the policy completes 225 consecutive 180° rotations (112.5 full rotations) at ~0.36 rot/s in a single continuous trial, without any vision or object-state information. This is the strongest reported hardware result for Baoding ball manipulation in both rotation count and speed, regardless of sensing modality. We further quantify the contribution of tactile sensing relative to proprioception alone, and identify the hand regions where tactile information matters most.

Method

  • Blind agent. Each observation frame holds 52 proprioceptive values (joint position, joint velocity, position error and previous action for the 13 actuators) and 16 binary tactile values; four frames are stacked into a 272-dimensional observation. The action is a 13-dimensional joint-position target issued at 60 Hz. Policies are trained with PPO in Isaac Lab on 10 s episodes (600 control steps), using the RoTO reward: a dense term for keeping the balls in the hand and a sparse term for each completed exchange.
  • Coupling-aware embodiment. The PIP and DIP joints of the index, middle and ring fingers are mechanically coupled, leaving 13 independently actuated joints. On hardware the coupling is sequential: during flexion the PIP moves first and the DIP only begins once the PIP reaches ~100°, with the order reversed on extension. The default Isaac Lab asset instead drives the DIP as a fixed multiple of the PIP. We apply the sequential coupling explicitly at every environment step, and add collision bodies at the physical sensor locations so simulated tactile coverage matches the hardware.
  • Physical randomisation. Ball mass is sampled from 45–100 g, and static and dynamic friction are randomised independently for each ball and for the distal, middle, proximal, knuckle and palm regions of the hand, with higher friction on the silicone-covered BioTac fingertips.
  • Slew-rate randomisation (SlewDR). Even with soft gains, the policy commanded joint motion faster than the hardware could execute, likely due to tendon friction and reflected motor inertia that the simulation does not model. We limit the change in commanded position per step, qt + clip(qdest − qt, ±s q̇max Δt), resampling the scale s ~ U(0.3, 1.0) at each episode reset in simulation and holding it fixed on hardware.
  • Stuck-AT Taxels (STAT). Policies trained on clean tactile input over-rely on individual channels, which hurts transfer when those channels misbehave. At each reset, k ~ U{0,…,6} of the 12 FSR channels are selected and each is frozen at a random value v ~ U{0,1} for the whole episode, while the rest report the true contact state. This acts like episode-level dropout, so the policy cannot trust any single taxel.
  • Hysteresis tactile calibration. Static FSR thresholds failed because the baseline reading shifts with hand pose and wire movement, even with an empty hand. Each channel gets its own upper and lower thresholds from its empty-hand baseline (τhi = P99.5 + 5; τlo just above the median, or 3 below τhi). Contact switches on above τhi and off below τlo, like a Schmitt trigger.
